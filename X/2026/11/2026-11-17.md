> I've been talking to a bunch of PhD students and researchers recently, and I've qualitatively noticed two growing (but concerning) trends with AI:
> 
> 1. Researchers seem to be reading less. Some even reported that they read <10 papers a year now with AI because they can simply either 1) retrieve or 2) summarize papers. BUT deep reading is how we engage with underlying intellectual arguments, develop research taste, spot methodological flaws, and form original ideas. It's arguably the most fun part of a PhD imo
> 
> 2. Researchers seem to be vibe-coding and paper-maxxing. AI dramatically cheapens execution, such as implementing experiments, running analyses, and producing manuscripts. Some even "let [Claude Code] rip" without checking the code or validating the results somehow. BUT it also incentivizes quantity over depth, rigor, and meaningful contributions.
> 
> Overall, the cost of producing research is falling much faster than the cost of developing genuine intellectual understanding. Rather than simply producing more papers, I think we should engage deeply with the literature, ask questions that truly matter, and produce work that meaningfully advances our collective understanding.

### @hannahhaina (Hannah Xu)
[Tweet](https://x.com/hannahhaina/status/2122703524589179118)

> What is a data company actually worth in the long run?
> 
> I've been asking lab researchers, data founders and some of the largest data buyers this for a few months. Here's where I've landed for now.
> 
> 1/ Frontier labs don't need more data. They need a small amount of really good data, then they synthesize and run RL on top of it. Volume gets cheap fast.
> 
> 2/ Expert networks feel this first. Writing and grading answers is exactly what models are getting good at, and in some domains I'm told synthetic data already beats the experts. The margins say the same thing: public numbers for human-data vendors sit around 30–45%, because most of the revenue goes straight to contractors.
> 
> 3/ RL environments have a few good years while verifiers are still hard. Over time I'd expect the value to move to QA, catching reward hacking and keeping sandboxes safe.
> 
> 4/ Real-world data looks like the actual moat to me. Archives, long-tail industries, live workflows, the physical world, wet-lab results: things the model can't reach on its own. Licensing that data many times over is a great business. Collecting it in the field is closer to running a supply chain.
> 
> 5/ The piece nobody has built yet is a Moody's for data. Today, checking a dataset can cost almost as much as making it, so buyers and sellers end up grading their own homework.
> 
> And most data revenue is still one-off projects. The companies that turn it into something recurring, like licenses, environments or verifiers, will be the real ones.
> 
> Curious where people disagree. If you run a data company or buy data at a lab, what would you still pay for after your next model ships? And is anyone actually close to building the Moody's?
