### @rohanpaul_ai (Rohan Paul)
[Tweet](https://x.com/rohanpaul_ai/status/2126431108158107964)

> A new paper from Stanford researchers shows that when every agent sees the full question, agent teams spend more tokens without beating independent agents that just vote.
> 
> So anyone building multi-agent systems should compare against a majority vote of independent agents at the same token cost, not against 1 agent.
> 
> Agent teams usually get compared with 1 agent, but a team burns many times the tokens. A plain majority vote over independent answers already explains most reported gains.
> 
> The researchers graded teams against independent agents with the same token budget: 1 agent, a majority vote, and pass@n, where any agent being right counts. They tested Qwen models on shared-question tasks and on tasks where each agent holds only some facts.
> 
> On 6 shared-question benchmarks, debate and 2 other multi-agent methods never beat the same-cost vote. With split facts, 4 Qwen3.8-27B agents forwarding their facts scored 0.959 accuracy, above a full-information agent at 0.942 with the same budget.
> 
> Before adding agents, check whether a same-cost vote does as well, and have agents share raw facts rather than conclusions.

### @SuJinyan6 (Jinyan Su)
[Tweet](https://x.com/SuJinyan6/status/2126373011297366376)

> Finally finished reading this. 🫡
> Some of the take-aways:
> 1. Those so called RSI mainly edit the harness, not rather than the weights. The harness is cheap to change but easy to overfit. In 21 settings where harness search clearly helped, one paper traced 54% of the gain to overfitting.😓
> 
> 2. The hard part is not proposing edits, but to judge whether these edits are good. Three papers show that the judging step can inflate the numbers. Another shows that whoever controls the judge controls which behaviors the agent keeps.
> 
> 3. The only reliable defense is a check the loop cannot optimize, such as a held-out sets, fresh items for scoring, labeled anchor examples, protected layers, human review, real users, and hidden tests.
> 
> 4. Humans have not left the loop. They moved from writing prompts to setting evals and reviewing edits.
> So near-term progress depends on how fast and how well people can build evals.
> 
> 5. RSI suits well on tasks where its metric is fast and can be trusted.
> 
> 6. Reporting standards are not unified yet, so check how a number was measured, and whether compute was matched, how many seeds were run, how the grader was checked and what it cost when reading these paper or PRs from some RSI start ups that constantly claiming that they are changing the world.

> One of the hardest parts of building RL environments for science is getting the reward function right.
> 
> With RetroEnv, our initial reward function was pretty straightforward, but the agent quickly learned to reward hack. Even bad solutions during evaluations were getting high rewards.

### @noahatwhere (NoaX)
[Tweet](https://x.com/noahatwhere/status/2126260499688083843)

> Been thinking about what actually comes after RL.
> 
> Right now RL works crazy well anywhere you can write a verifier. Math, code, tool use. If you can grade the answer, you can throw compute at it and climb all the way to superhuman.
> 
> The catch is most real work doesn't have a unit test. You don't get a clean +1 for a good product call or a research direction. Feedback is noisy and usually shows up months later.
> 
> My bet is the next real jump is continual learning. Models today are still frozen snapshots that forget everything the second a session ends. Things only get truly weird once they can learn on the job from messy, delayed feedback instead of waiting for us to build them a sandbox and a rubric.
> 
> Until then, we're mostly just getting insanely good at training test-takers.
